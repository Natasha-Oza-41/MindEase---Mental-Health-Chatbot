# Crisis Resources and How to Access Help

## Understanding a Mental Health Crisis
A mental health crisis is any situation in which a person's behaviour puts them at risk of hurting themselves or others, or prevents them from being able to care for themselves. Crises can include:
- Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
- A severe panic attack or dissociative episode
- A psychotic break (loss of contact with reality)
- Severe substance intoxication with risk of harm
- Complete inability to function

**A crisis is a medical emergency. It deserves the same urgency as a physical emergency.**

## If You Are Having Thoughts of Suicide

First: **you are not alone.** Suicidal thoughts are more common than most people realise, and they do not have to be acted upon. They are a signal that your current pain exceeds your current coping resources — not a statement about your future.

**Safety steps:**
1. **Tell someone** — a trusted friend, family member, or call a crisis line.
2. **Remove means** — if you have access to medications or other means, ask someone to secure them.
3. **Go somewhere safe** — be with other people; do not isolate.
4. **Call 988 or go to an ER** — if you feel you cannot keep yourself safe.

**Warning signs in yourself that need immediate attention:**
- Thinking about suicide frequently or making plans
- Giving away prized possessions
- Saying goodbye to people
- Sudden calmness after a period of severe depression (can indicate a decision has been made)

## How to Help Someone in Crisis

**DO:**
- Take all mentions of suicide seriously
- Ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" — asking does not plant the idea; it opens the door
- Listen without judgment
- Stay with them or arrange for someone to be with them
- Help them contact a crisis resource
- Remove access to means if possible

**DO NOT:**
- Promise to keep it secret
- Leave them alone if you believe the risk is high
- Argue about whether their reasons are "valid"
- Challenge or minimise their pain
- Say "you have so much to live for" or similar dismissive phrases

## After the Acute Crisis: Getting Ongoing Support

A crisis is often a turning point. After immediate safety is established:
- **Follow up with a mental health professional** (psychiatrist, psychologist, therapist)
- **Consider a safety plan** — a written, personalized plan of warning signs, coping strategies, and people to contact (ask your provider about Stanley-Brown Safety Planning)
- **Tell trusted people** what you are going through so they can support you
- **Remove or secure means** at home as a long-term safety measure
- **Maintain regular appointments** even (especially) when feeling better

## Online Support Communities
These are peer-support communities, not crisis lines. They are useful for connection and shared experience, but are not a substitute for professional help.
- 7 Cups (7cups.com) — trained volunteer listeners, 24/7
- Reddit r/mentalhealth — community support
- NAMI Community (nami.org) — National Alliance on Mental Illness forums

## Key Reminder
Reaching out for help during a crisis is an act of strength, not weakness. Every call to a crisis line, every visit to an emergency room, every text to a trusted friend is a courageous act of self-preservation. Help is available, and things can and do get better.
